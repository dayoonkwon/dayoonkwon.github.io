Exposures that occur decades apart from disease onset are hard to study with questionnaires and monitors alone. Molecular biomarkers offer a record of exposure that the body keeps. At Stanford, I develop and apply epigenetic biomarkers and aging clocks to measure how environmental exposures become biologically embedded and whether they accelerate aging.

One example comes from a natural experiment in northern Chile, where municipal drinking water carried very high arsenic concentrations from 1958 to 1972. Decades later, adults with higher arsenic exposure at birth showed older epigenetic age, and associations with phenotypic age and pace of aging were stronger among people with lower socioeconomic status. Current arsenic exposure, in contrast, was associated with methylation at specific CpG sites {% cite kwon2026_arsenic_epiage %}. Early-life and current exposures therefore leave different molecular signatures, and social conditions modify them.

Ongoing projects extend this approach to air pollution, wildfire smoke, and heat, and to DNA methylation biomarkers of cardiometabolic health in large cohorts. The goal is a set of exposure-sensitive biomarkers that connect the environment to aging and to disease in specific organ systems, including the cardiovascular system and the brain.
