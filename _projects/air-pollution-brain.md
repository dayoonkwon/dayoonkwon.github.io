Air pollution is one of the few risk factors for neurodegeneration that nearly everyone shares and that policy can change. I study how long-term exposure to traffic-related air pollution contributes to Parkinson's disease (PD), and which biological pathways carry that effect from the air into the brain.

My work draws on the Parkinson's Environment and Genes (PEG) study, a population-based case-control study in California's Central Valley with lifetime residential and workplace address histories. In PEG, long-term exposure to traffic-related air pollution was associated with higher odds of PD {% cite kwon2024_ap_pd %}. Genetic susceptibility amplified this association. In a meta-analysis of PEG and a Danish study, people with both a high PD polygenic risk score and high traffic-related exposure had about 3 times the odds of PD compared with people who had neither {% cite kwon2025_ap_prs_pd %}.

Criteria pollutants such as NO<sub>2</sub> and PM<sub>2.5</sub> cannot identify which constituents drive these associations. I therefore examined specific traffic-related air toxics and found the most consistent associations for nickel, with shorter-term workplace associations for benzene and 1,3-butadiene {% cite kwon2026_airtoxics_pd %}.

To understand mechanisms, I used untargeted serum metabolomics. Air pollution exposure was associated with inflammation-related lipid metabolism, membrane phospholipid remodeling, amino acid metabolism, and lower tyrosine, a precursor of dopamine, which points to inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction as plausible pathways {% cite kwon2026_ap_metab_pd %}. A narrative review summarizes the state of research on air pollution and neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD), and the methodological challenges ahead, including exposure windows earlier in life, PM<sub>2.5</sub> constituents and emerging sources such as wildfire smoke, and comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease {% cite kwon2025_ap_neuro_review %}.
